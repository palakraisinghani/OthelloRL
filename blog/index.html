Teaching a Machine to Play Othello

From hand-written bots to classic reinforcement learning to AlphaZero: what it takes for a program to learn a game where grabbing the most discs is usually the wrong move.

Othello's rules fit on a napkin. Place a disc so that it traps a line of your opponent's discs between two of yours, and every trapped disc flips to your colour. Whoever owns more discs when the board fills up wins.

That last sentence is a trap. Owning the most discs only matters at the very end. In the middle of the game it is usually a weakness: every disc you own is a disc your opponent can flip back, and every disc you flip opens up more places for them to move. Beginners grab. Strong players hold back, guard the corners and quietly starve their opponent of moves.

That makes Othello a lovely test for a learning machine. The only feedback, win or lose, arrives after dozens of moves, and the obvious stand-in for it points the wrong way. Can a program discover the real structure of the game from nothing but wins and losses?

This post walks through what I found while building a ladder of Othello players: hand-written bots that never learn, five classic reinforcement-learning methods, three self-play methods, and a few experiments on a question I didn't expect to care about this much: who makes a good practice partner? The strongest agent, an AlphaZero-style player I named Oroletto, is the engine behind every difficulty level of an Othello app I'm building. The game itself is already online to play.

The short version

  • Classic RL methods each hit a different wall: a table too big to fill, a learner that memorises its opponent, a model too simple to improve. None comes close to a bot that simply looks four moves ahead.
  • Who you practise against matters as much as how you learn. A fixed opponent becomes a frozen target; self-play keeps every game informative.
  • Search is the breakthrough. AlphaZero beats the look-ahead bot at its own game, and even its network on its own, with no search at all, plays as well as that bot.
  • Variety alone doesn't help. Weak opponents teach little, and a population of copies of yourself teaches the same thing as one copy.
  • The recipe scales to the full 8×8 board, and one trained model gives an app four difficulty levels just by changing how adventurously it picks its moves.

Primer

Reinforcement learning in two minutes

Reinforcement learning (RL) is learning by trial and error. An agent looks at the current situation (the state), picks an action, and the environment answers with a new state and, sometimes, a reward. Over many attempts the agent adjusts its behaviour, its policy, to collect as much reward as it can.

In Othello the state is the board, an action is a move, and the reward is +1 for a win, −1 for a loss and 0 for a draw, handed out only when the game ends. Two ideas do most of the work in everything below:

  • A value says how good a position, or a move in a position, is: the reward you can expect from there on. Learn good values and good moves follow.
  • Credit assignment is the puzzle of figuring out which of thirty moves earned the win when the only feedback is the final result. Most of what makes RL hard, and most of what separates the methods in this post, comes down to how they solve it.

Board games are a favourite playground because the simulator is perfect and free, so you can play millions of games, and because an agent can practise against copies of itself. The field's history is largely a history of games:

01A game you can learn in a minute

Most of this project runs on a 6×6 board, where games are short and experiments are cheap, before moving to the standard 8×8 board at the end. The rules don't change with the size.

  • A move must capture. You place a disc so that one or more of your opponent's discs lie in a straight line between it and another of your discs: across, down or diagonally.
  • Captured discs flip to your colour, along every line the new disc closes at once.
  • No legal move means you pass, and your opponent moves again. You can never pass by choice.
  • The game ends when the board is full or neither side can move. Most discs wins.

What good players know

Four ideas come back again and again in this post, both in how the bots are built and in what the learners do or fail to discover.

Corners are forever

No line can ever trap a corner disc, so it can never be flipped. A corner also lets the discs along its edges become permanent.

Next to a corner is poison

The square diagonally next to an empty corner (the X-square) usually hands that corner to your opponent.

Mobility is power

Keep many moves available to yourself and few to your opponent, and eventually they are forced onto the squares that give corners away.

The disc-count trap

Lots of discs mid-game means lots of discs to be flipped back and lots of moves handed to your opponent. Only the final count matters.

One more fact turns out to be useful later. Both boards used here are solved. On 6×6, perfect play gives White, the second player, a 20–16 win. On 8×8, perfect play was shown in 2023 to end in a draw. The game is still far too big to look up moves in a table, so it remains a genuine learning problem.

02Three players that never learn

Before training anything I built three opponents from hand-written rules. They are the yardsticks for everything that follows, so they need to form a clear ladder.

The random bot plays any legal move. It is the floor: anything that has learned something should beat it.

The heuristic bot looks exactly one move ahead. For every legal move it scores the resulting position with a weighted sum of four features (corners, mobility, disc count and X-squares, each as "mine minus yours") and plays the best one. I picked the weights by a grid search, playing 96 different weightings against a weak, a medium and a strong reference bot.

The grid search measured the disc-count trap directly. Corners carry almost all of the heuristic's strength and mobility comes second. Disc count contributes almost nothing, and a bot that only counts discs is barely better than random.

The minimax bot adds lookahead. It searches several moves deep, assuming both sides always play their best reply, and scores the positions at the end of the search with the heuristic. A standard trick called alpha-beta pruning skips branches that can't change the decision.

The first couple of extra moves of lookahead buy most of the strength; after that each extra move costs several times more search for a smaller gain. I settled on minimax at depth 4 as the reference opponent: past the steep part of the curve, and cheap enough to play thousands of evaluation games. Random, heuristic and minimax-d4 form a strict ladder on 6×6, 8×8 and 10×10 boards, which is exactly what a yardstick needs.

Method note

How I keep score

Every match starts from a set of random six-move openings, and each opening is played twice with the colours swapped, so the first-move advantage cancels out. The score counts a win as 1 and a draw as ½, from the first-named player's side, and comes with a 95% confidence interval. Every learner is trained with three independent random seeds.

03Classic RL hits three different walls

The first learners are the textbook methods. Each one trains for 200,000 games against the heuristic bot, starting from random openings and switching colours every game, and then plays all three yardstick bots.

Q-learning

A lookup table with one value per position and move, nudged towards the reward plus its own estimate of the next position.

SARSA

The same table, but it learns about the moves it actually plays, exploration included.

Monte Carlo control

Waits until the game ends, then moves every value it visited towards the actual result.

Linear features

A value is a weighted sum of six hand-picked features, so similar positions get similar values.

DQN

A neural network reads the raw board and learns its own features.

Nothing beats the minimax bot, and the methods fail in strikingly different ways. The learning curves show the three walls.

Wall 1: the table is too big

Q-learning and SARSA never get past random play. After 200,000 games their tables hold well over a million positions, yet when they meet the random bot, fewer than one in ten of the positions they face is in the table at all. Othello has far too many positions to learn one at a time. The two methods also behave identically, which surprised me until I saw why: they differ only in which next-position value they learn from, and that position is almost always one they have never seen, so both read the same default zero.

Wall 2: memorising the opponent

Monte Carlo control is the odd one out. It beats the heuristic bot it trained against, yet against the random bot, the easiest opponent there is, it does no better than a coin flip. Something that had learned Othello would find random play the easiest of all. Instead, against a nearly predictable opponent the same lines of play keep coming back, and Monte Carlo control memorised a growing book of winning lines against that one bot. I had added random openings and an occasional random move to the heuristic bot precisely to prevent this. It wasn't enough.

Wall 3: a model too simple to improve

The linear learner generalises: similar positions get similar values whether it has seen them or not, so it beats the random bot almost immediately. Then it stops. The best thing a weighted sum of those six features can express is a re-weighted version of the heuristic bot, and it finds those weights within a few thousand games. More data can't fix a model that has nothing better to say.

DQN: the best of them, and still blind to the future

DQN combines the missing pieces: it generalises like the linear model, but it learns its own features from the raw board, and a replay buffer and a slowly updated target network keep its learning stable. It is the only classic method that clearly beats its training opponent without forgetting how to play everyone else. But it still picks each move from a single glance at the board, with no lookahead, and the minimax bot punishes exactly the tactical oversights that a one-glance player can't see.

Every classic learner chooses its move from what the board looks like right now. None of them learns to look ahead, and that, not the amount of training, is what separates them from a simple search bot.

04A fixed opponent is a frozen target

All the classic learners practised against the same heuristic bot. Was that holding DQN back? To find out, I trained identical DQNs against three different fixed opponents (the random bot, the heuristic bot and minimax looking three moves ahead) and one more that only ever played itself.

The naive expectation is a ceiling: a learner can't get stronger than its teacher. That's wrong. The classic DQN already beats its own trainer most of the time. The real limit is subtler.

The learner trained against the random bot hits its final level almost immediately and stays there. Once you beat an opponent nearly every game, the games stop saying anything about which moves mattered. That is the frozen target. The heuristic-trained learner still improves, but slowly. Only the learner facing the strongest bot is still climbing at the end, because it still loses often enough for each game to carry information.

There is a second effect. Against the rule-based bots, strength follows the trainer, with the minimax-trained learner on top. But when the learners play each other, that advantage disappears.

Minimax and the heuristic bot share the same evaluation function, so a learner trained against one of them gets very good at beating that particular style of play, which flatters it against that family of bots and nobody else. The self-play learner never met a single bot during training, and it beats every fixed-opponent learner head-to-head.

A fixed opponent doesn't cap a learner, but it freezes its target and narrows what it learns to one style. An opponent that improves along with the learner fixes both problems. That opponent is yourself.

05Self-play, and why search changes everything

Self-play means the learner's opponent is itself, or saved copies of its earlier selves. No hand-written opponent is needed, and the opponent's strength always matches the learner's. I compared three self-play methods, all trained without ever seeing a rule-based bot:

  • DQN+: DQN with a fix for its habit of overestimating values (Double DQN), playing a pool of its own past snapshots half the time.
  • PPO: a policy-gradient method that learns a policy directly and keeps each update small, a workhorse of modern RL.
  • AlphaZero: a neural network combined with a tree search that looks ahead before every move. It gets its own section next.

Self-play without search lands right where the best fixed-opponent learner did. That's a real result: DQN+ and PPO reach that strength without ever meeting the heuristic bot, and neither shows Monte Carlo's "beats its trainer but not a random player" failure. But they share the same ceiling, which I attribute to what they have in common: one glance at the board per move.

AlphaZero is in a different league. It is the first agent in this project that clearly beats the minimax bot, and the two AlphaZero bars show that search helps it in two separate ways:

  • Search at play time. Letting the same network look ahead before each move adds a large margin, most of all against minimax. Search does what minimax does: it checks the replies, which removes the blunders a one-glance player can't see.
  • Search as a teacher. Even with no search at all, AlphaZero's network plays as well as minimax-d4, where the DQN+ and PPO networks win about one game in five. The difference is what it was trained on: the results of a lookahead search, not raw game outcomes one move at a time.

One caveat I couldn't resolve: AlphaZero's network is a convolutional network, which sees the board as a grid, while DQN+ and PPO use plain fully connected layers. Part of that second gap could come from the architecture rather than the training signal, and separating the two would need another run.

Per game, AlphaZero is much more expensive, since every move runs a search guided by the network. Even so, it passed a level within its first twenty minutes of training that PPO never reached in its full run. Its learning curve against the heuristic bot also saturates quickly, which is a limitation of that yardstick rather than of the learner: once you win almost every game, the curve can't show further progress.

Aside

Does it know who wins?

6×6 Othello is solved: with perfect play from the start, White wins 20–16. When asked to evaluate the starting position, the trained network says clearly that Black loses, and when it plays itself from the start, White wins every game. It does not play perfectly, though. White always wins by more than four discs, which means Black is making mistakes. It has learned who wins, not yet exactly how.

Of the three AlphaZero training runs, the one that scored best against the minimax bot became the project's final 6×6 model: Oroletto.

06How AlphaZero decides on a move

AlphaZero has two parts. A neural network provides fast intuition: given a board, it outputs a probability for every legal move (the prior, "which moves look promising") and a value between −1 and +1 ("who is winning"). A tree search then checks that intuition by looking ahead.

The search

Before every move, AlphaZero runs a fixed number of simulations. Each simulation walks down a tree of possible continuations, at each step choosing the move that maximises

until it reaches a position it hasn't seen yet. The network evaluates that position once, and its value is passed back up the path, flipping sign at each step because the players alternate. N(s,a) counts how often a move has been tried, and Q(s,a) is the average value of every simulation that went through it. The bonus term is large for moves the network likes but the search has rarely tried, and it shrinks as a move gets checked. Early on the search follows the network's intuition; as evidence piles up, it follows the evidence.

Most visits, not the highest average

After the search, AlphaZero plays the most-visited move, not the move with the highest average value. That sounds like a detail, but it matters. A move's average is built from network evaluations, and with only a few visits it is noisy. If you pick the largest of several noisy averages, you will tend to pick the move whose estimate happens to be too high. A visit count can't be inflated that way: the search keeps sending simulations to a move only while its average holds up under repeated checking. Why averaging makes the search forgiving

The minimax bot backs up the best value at every branch. If a single far-off position is misjudged as too good, that one error gets selected and carried straight to the top. AlphaZero's search backs up averages instead, so a position is judged by many network evaluations rather than by its single most optimistic one, and independent mistakes partly cancel out. As simulations concentrate on the best replies, those averages drift towards what minimax would compute, so nothing is lost in the limit. With a small budget and an imperfect network, the averaging is what keeps the search stable, and it is why a few dozen simulations are enough to outplay a four-move minimax search.

Learning from its own searches

Here is the clever part. Every position AlphaZero meets in self-play is saved with two training targets: the visit distribution of its search (π, how the simulations were spread over the moves) and the final result of the game (z). The network is trained to predict both:

In the example above, the search gave c5 a much bigger share of its visits than the network's prior did. Training pulls the prior that way, so the next search starts out already favouring c5. The search improves on the network, the network learns to be what the search was, and the next search starts from a better place.

07Does variety make a better teacher?

Self-play always faces an opponent of exactly the learner's strength and style. A popular alternative, used by DeepMind's AlphaStar, is a league: a varied population of opponents, so that the learner can't overfit to any one style. I tested two versions of that idea with DQN on 6×6. Both said no, and the reasons why are the interesting part.

A league of weak opponents makes things worse

The first experiment mixed a league of fixed opponents (random, the heuristic bot, minimax, the classic tabular learners and the learner's own past snapshots) into DQN's self-play. Everything else was held fixed. Only the share of games played against the league changed, from zero (pure self-play) to all of them.

Strength falls steadily as the league's share grows, against every held-out opponent, and, most surprisingly, even against the league bots the learner actually trained against. The training logs explain it. Most league members are beaten nearly every game from the start, and the strongest beats the learner most of the time. Games that are almost certainly won or almost certainly lost say very little about which moves made the difference. Self-play gives an evenly matched, informative game every single time. Replacing those games with lopsided ones slows learning down.

A population of equals changes nothing

So maybe variety helps if the opponents are evenly matched? The second experiment trained populations of 2, 4 or 8 independent DQNs that learn only by playing each other: always adaptive, always roughly as strong as each other, never playing a copy of themselves.

Given the same number of games per agent, a population member ends up exactly as strong as a self-play learner. Its strength depends on how many games it has played, not on whom it played. Split a fixed budget across a population and each member simply gets less practice, so it ends up weaker. The members also disagree with each other about moves no more than three separately trained self-play runs do, and there are no rock-paper-scissors cycles among them. A peer trained by the same recipe is just another sample of the same player.

Weak opponents hurt because their games teach little, not because they add variety. Variety from equals doesn't hurt, but it doesn't help either when the equals are copies of the same recipe. For a league to beat self-play, its members need to be genuinely different and close to the learner's strength, such as dedicated "exploiters" whose only job is to find the learner's weaknesses.

08Scaling up to the real board

Everything so far ran on 6×6. The board people actually play is 8×8, so the last question was whether the recipe carries over. I first re-tuned the bots for the bigger board. The best weights turned out to be identical to the 6×6 ones, which killed my theory that the heuristic bot struggles on bigger boards because its weights were tuned for a small one. The real reason is that longer games give lookahead more chances to pay off. On 8×8, minimax-d4 beats the heuristic bot almost every game.

That creates a measuring problem: once the best yardstick is beaten almost every time, it can't tell strong agents apart. So the 8×8 agent was also measured against Edax, a free, world-class Othello engine whose search depth can be dialled from weak to formidable.

The AlphaZero recipe stayed the same, with a deeper network (six residual blocks instead of four) and more search per move (150 simulations instead of 64), trained for about 100,000 self-play games over roughly two days on an 8-core CPU.

Both roles of search reappear. With search, the agent beats minimax-d4 from the very first checkpoint and approaches the top of the scale. The network alone improves steadily all the way through and ends up clearly ahead of minimax-d4 without any search at all.

Against Edax, the score falls steadily as Edax searches deeper: a clear win at level 3, a narrow loss at level 5. That puts the agent somewhere between those two, a respectable middle setting of an engine that is far stronger at full depth. Edax's evaluation was fitted to a large collection of expert games, and at high levels it searches much deeper than 150 network-guided simulations can.

Behind the scenes

The bug that cost two days

The first 8×8 training run slowed down about seven-fold after around ten thousand games, for no visible reason. The culprit was weight decay: it had pushed thousands of network weights so close to zero that they became denormal floating-point numbers, a special range of tiny values that CPUs process more than ten times more slowly. One line to flush them to zero fixed it, and the run started over from scratch. If a long CPU training run mysteriously crawls, check for denormals.

These 8×8 results come from one training run. Two more runs with different seeds are training as I write this, to check how reproducible the strength is; if either turns out clearly stronger, it will replace the current model.

09Four difficulty levels from one brain

The 8×8 model is the engine of the Othello app I'm building, which needs easy, medium, hard and master levels. Training four separate models would be wasteful and hard to keep consistent. Instead, every level uses the same model and the same search, and changes only how the move is picked from the search's visit counts, using a temperature T: each move is chosen with probability proportional to N1/T. At T = 0 it always plays the most-visited move. As T rises, it spreads its choices over the other moves its search considered.

Every level beats the one below it comfortably, and the levels line up with familiar opponents: Easy plays about as well as the heuristic bot, Hard beats the minimax bot, and Master is the full-strength model. Easy is not a true beginner, though, since it only ever chooses among moves its search took seriously. Making a gentler level would need a different knob, such as less search.

Shipping the model meant exporting the network to a portable format (ONNX) along with a small manifest describing its inputs, search settings and levels, plus a set of test positions with the exact visit counts the search must produce. The app reimplements the search itself, and because search without random noise is deterministic, those counts must match to the last visit. A move takes well under a second.

10What I learned

  • The obvious reward signal can point the wrong way. Counting discs is the beginner's mistake, and the disc-only bot proved it numerically before any learning began.
  • Each classic method fails for its own reason: too many positions to tabulate, memorising one opponent, or a model too simple to say anything new. Knowing why a method stalls is more useful than knowing that it does.
  • The best practice partner is one at your own level that keeps improving. Fixed opponents freeze, weak ones teach little, and identical peers teach nothing new. Self-play gets this right by construction.
  • Lookahead is the dividing line. Search helps twice: when choosing a move, and as a teacher whose output the network learns to imitate.
  • Small engineering details decide big runs. Pairing every opening with both colours, holding out evaluation opponents, and one denormal-number bug each mattered as much as the choice of algorithm.

What's next

Next up: finishing the extra 8×8 training runs, a league that samples opponents near the learner's own strength (rather than weak ones), an agent that watches a specific opponent and learns to exploit its weaknesses, and move-by-move feedback for people playing the app, flagging the moves where the model's evaluation says they gave away the most.
